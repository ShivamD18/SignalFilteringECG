The ECG signal

What the electrodes actually measure

Each heartbeat starts as an electrical wave that spreads through the heart muscle. Electrodes on the skin pick up a millivolt-sized shadow of it: the electrocardiogram.

  • P wave: the atria depolarise and contract.
  • QRS complex: the ventricles depolarise. It is tall and fast (80-120 ms), which is why it is the easiest thing to detect.
  • T wave: the ventricles repolarise and recover.

MIT-BIH was recorded on ambulatory (Holter) monitors at Boston's Beth Israel Hospital, digitised at 360 samples per second with 11-bit resolution over a 10 mV range. Two or more cardiologists labelled all ~110,000 beats, which is what lets us measure how good an algorithm is.

The pink grid is the same as clinical ECG paper: a small square is 40 ms x 0.1 mV, a big square 200 ms x 0.5 mV.

Butterworth

Maximally flat in the passband. Higher order means a steeper roll-off, but also more ringing and phase distortion.

Zero-phase vs causal

A real-time monitor can only use past samples, so its filter delays some frequencies more than others. Offline software runs the filter forward and then backward (filtfilt), which cancels the phase shift and doubles the attenuation.

Why 0.5 Hz is a compromise

Clinical guidelines (AHA 2007) allow a 0.67 Hz high-pass only if it is zero-phase; otherwise use 0.05 Hz. A causal high-pass above that bends the ST segment, the part doctors read for heart attacks.

Try this: . The ST segment after each QRS sags: a filter-made "ST depression". Switch to zero-phase and it disappears. Then : the P and T waves vanish and the R wave shrinks, yet the detector in lesson 4 still works, because Pan-Tompkins uses exactly this band.

Finding every heartbeat

The Pan-Tompkins algorithm (1985)

Before classifying beats we have to find them. Pan and Tompkins' detector is still the standard baseline, and this page runs it on your filtered signal from lesson 3:

  1. Band-pass 5-15 Hz keeps the QRS energy and rejects P/T waves, baseline and hum.
  2. Derivative emphasises the steep slopes of the QRS.
  3. Squaring makes everything positive and boosts big slopes.
  4. Moving-window integration (150 ms) merges each QRS into one smooth hump.
  5. Adaptive thresholds track running estimates of signal and noise peaks, search back for missed beats, and reject T waves by their gentler slope.

Classifying beats with machine learning

Normal or abnormal, and which kind

Each detected beat becomes a row of 46 numbers (features). A machine-learning model turns them into one of four classes defined by the AAMI EC57 standard:

    The features fall into three groups:

    • Rhythm (RR intervals): did the beat come early, and was there a pause after it? An ectopic beat usually arrives early and is followed by a compensatory pause.
    • Template comparison: how different is the beat from this patient's own median beat? Ventricular beats look wide and strange.
    • Morphology: 26 amplitude-normalised samples of the waveform.
